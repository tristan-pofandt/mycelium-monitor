# Turn factory telemetry into audit-ready evidence

RegTech ("regulatory technology") is software that helps an organisation follow the laws and rules that apply to it, and prove to an outsider that it did. It is a real, funded and fast-consolidating market of roughly **USD 18-26 billion in 2026** on the narrow vendor-revenue measure, and most of it is crowded: security-certification automation (Vanta alone is near **USD 300 million in annual recurring revenue**), anti-money-laundering checks, carbon accounting, regulatory-change content and enterprise risk platforms all have well-funded leaders. For a solo Australian founder whose product is a compliance plug-in driven by Mycelium, the realistic opening is narrow but genuine: a **cross-vendor, multi-site evidence layer** that turns data Mycelium already collects (door and camera metadata, device posture from Intune, rosters, people records) into dated, per-site, per-control proof, mapped to free Australian content (the ASD Essential Eight, SOCI risk-program hazards) and to ISO 27001 control numbers, and pushed into the risk tools a manufacturer already owns rather than replacing them. One earlier finding needed correcting, and the report follows the correction throughout: products that turn physical access data into compliance evidence **already exist for large enterprises** (AlertEnterprise, HID SAFE, RightCrowd, Genetec ClearID), and Gallagher sites in Australia can already lock a door when someone's licence lapses. So "nobody does this" is false. What looks unserved is the mid-market, mixed-vendor, multi-site version that goes beyond "who holds which badge" to camera uptime, alarms, visitor logs and inspections, joined to cyber and people evidence. Much of the gap evidence is "I searched and found nothing", which is weaker than proof, no one has sized this niche, and whether auditors will accept automated physical evidence is untested. The sensible first step is a read-only evidence pack for one design-partner site, with AI drafting and named people approving, worded as "test results", never as "you are compliant".

## RegTech is compliance software that has outgrown banking

### What the words mean

A **regulation** is a rule set by a government body, and the body that enforces it is a **regulator** (in Australia, for example, ASIC for companies and markets, APRA for banks and insurers, AUSTRAC for money laundering, the OAIC for privacy, and state safety regulators such as SafeWork NSW). **Compliance** is the everyday work of following those rules and keeping the proof. Think of a car: obeying road rules is compliance, and the logbook of services is the proof you would show a buyer or an inspector. RegTech is any software that makes either half of that job cheaper or more reliable.

The term became official policy language in the UK. HM Treasury's March 2015 Budget asked the Financial Conduct Authority to support "so-called 'RegTech'", and the FCA's November 2015 call for input drew more than 350 responses ([FCA RegTech page](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/innovation/regtech); [FCA FS16/4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/feedback/fs-16-04.pdf)). The popular line that "the FCA coined RegTech" is a simplification: no source proves who first used the word. It began inside **FinTech** (technology that delivers financial services, such as payment apps and online lenders) because banks, hit by the heavy rules that followed the 2008 financial crisis, were the first big buyers. Juniper Research now says RegTech spending "expands beyond just financial services" ([The Stack](https://thestack.technology/regtech-spending-triples-by-2026)). Two neighbouring terms cause confusion. **SupTech** ("supervisory technology") is the regulator's side of the same coin: tools regulators use to watch firms. **GRC** (governance, risk and compliance) is an older software category, a central register of risks, rules, policies, audits and owners. Market trackers now count GRC as a segment inside RegTech; the biggest RegTech deal of early 2025 by one tracker's count was Protecht, a Sydney GRC company that raised **USD 280 million** ([RegTech Analyst](https://regtechanalyst.com/?p=82030)). The trackers also count cyber-security evidence firms such as Vanta and Armis, because cyber rules turned "prove your security works" into a compliance product. The boundary is set by whoever is counting, and that matters to the founder for a practical reason: headline market numbers include segments the founder will never sell into.

A handful of words recur through the rest of this report. A **control** is one thing an organisation promises to do, such as "only people with a badge can enter the comms room". A **framework** is a published list of controls, such as ISO 27001 (an international information-security standard you can be certified against) or the Essential Eight (eight baseline cyber defences published free by the Australian Signals Directorate, ASD). **Evidence** is the proof an auditor will accept, such as a door-log export plus a manager's monthly sign-off. An **audit** is an independent check by an **auditor**, and an **attestation** is a person formally stating "I checked this" and attaching proof, for controls software cannot test. **Continuous control monitoring** means software checks the evidence automatically and often, instead of a person checking once a year.

### How big it is depends on who counts

Vendor-revenue estimates for 2026 cluster at about **USD 17.5-26 billion** with forecast growth of roughly **17-22% a year** (the "compound annual growth rate", or CAGR, the steady yearly rate that would produce the forecast): Fortune Business Insights says USD 23.43 billion in 2026 ([Fortune Business Insights](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/segmentation/regtech-market-108305)), Polaris USD 20.79 billion ([Polaris](https://www.polarismarketresearch.com/industry-analysis/regtech-market)), 360iResearch USD 17.54 billion ([GII](https://www.giiresearch.com/report/ires2000574-regtech-market-by-component-enterprise-size.html)) and Grand View USD 24.34 billion for 2025 ([MarketResearch.com](https://www.marketresearch.com/Grand-View-Research-v4060/RegTech-44620628/)). Juniper's much larger figures (USD 83 billion of "spend" in 2023) count organisations' internal technology budgets for compliance too ([ITWeb](https://itweb.co.za/content/R8OKdWqDkgoMbznQ)). Neither is wrong; they measure different things. Australia is roughly **USD 0.3-0.9 billion** depending on the firm ([Grand View Horizon](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/horizon/outlook/regtech-market/australia); [MarkNtel](https://www.marknteladvisors.com/research-library/regtech-market-australia.html)). No free source gives a reliable split between financial services and other industries, so the common claim that banks are "most" of the market is an inference, not a measured fact.

Money is flowing back after a 2023-2024 trough, but even the totals disagree: KPMG counts **USD 4.9 billion** of RegTech investment in 2025 ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/lv/en/insights/2026/02/pulse-of-fintech.html)), FinTech Global more than USD 5 billion with Australia fourth globally at USD 280 million ([FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/08/05/over-5bn-raised-by-regtech-companies-in-2025/)), and RegTech Analyst USD 8.5 billion ([RegTech Analyst](https://regtechanalyst.com/?p=83976)). Australia's figure is essentially one deal (Protecht), so it is not a trend. The money is going to three places: AI that does compliance work ("agentic" tools, meaning AI that carries out multi-step tasks rather than only answering questions, such as Norm Ai's **USD 120 million** Series C in July 2026 ([Under30CEO](https://www.under30ceo.com/compliance-software-norm-ai-series-c/))), cyber and trust management, and identity and fraud checks.

The exits are telling. A startup's **exit** is how its investors get paid, usually by being bought (**M&A**, mergers and acquisitions) or listing on a stock exchange. In RegTech it is almost all acquisitions: ServiceNow bought Armis for about **USD 7.75 billion** (closed April 2026) ([TelcoNews AU](https://telconews.com.au/story/servicenow-completes-usd-7-75-billion-armis-acquisition)); Bloomberg Industry Group bought Regology in June 2026 ([Windsor Drake](https://windsordrake.com/market-intelligence/transactions/regology-bloomberg-industry-group-2026)); private-equity-backed CUBE has bought Thomson Reuters' regulatory intelligence business, Reg-Room, Acin and 4CRisk.ai ([PrivSource](https://www.privsource.com/acquisitions/deal/7YSLxm); [A-Team Insight](https://a-teaminsight.com/briefs/cube-acquires-silicon-valley-regtech-4crisk-delivering-next-generation-compliance-and-risk-mapping-automation/)); Riskonnect bought Melbourne's Camms in 2024 to grow in Asia Pacific ([Riskonnect](https://riskonnect.com/press/riskonnect-acquires-camms-grc/)). For the founder this says two things: big platforms buy specialist data and connectors they lack, and an Australian niche product can be valuable to a global platform. Being "the connector the platform is missing" is a credible end state.

### Australia is an outsized hub with a 2026 wave of deadlines

A 2020 BCG study with the RegTech Association called Australia the world's **third-largest RegTech hub**, with about 80 of roughly 600 RegTechs worldwide ([Consultancy.com.au](https://www.consultancy.com.au/news/2682/australia-the-worlds-third-largest-regtech-hub-says-bcg)); that figure is six years old. The local tailwind is regulation: APRA's operational-risk standard CPS 230 took effect on 1 July 2025 ([APRA CPS 230](https://www.apra.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-07/Prudential%20Standard%20CPS%20230%20Operational%20Risk%20Management%20-%20clean.pdf)); anti-money-laundering "Tranche 2" brought lawyers, accountants and real-estate agents in from 1 July 2026 ([Legl](https://legl.com/articles/tranche-2-is-live-what-the-1-july-start-date-means-for-australian-law-firms)); mandatory climate reporting reached "Group 2" companies from 1 July 2026 ([Anthesis](https://www.anthesisgroup.com/au/insights/asrs-and-aasb-s2-a-guide-to-mandatory-climate-reporting-in-australia/)); and new privacy-policy rules on automated decisions apply from **10 December 2026** ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making)). Not all of these touch manufacturers, which the next sections sort out.

## Everything a company could want from RegTech comes down to ten jobs

### The segments, in plain words

The table below lists the main jobs RegTech does, an everyday example of each, and how open each one is to a newcomer like this founder. The "crowding" and "fit" columns are this report's judgement from the evidence, not a sourced ranking.

| Segment | What it does, in plain words | Everyday example | Crowding | Fit with Mycelium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identity checks (KYC, "know your customer") | Confirms a customer is who they say, from ID documents, selfies and registries | A bank app asking for a driver's licence photo and a selfie | Very crowded; priced per check at about USD 0.80-3.80 ([Tech-Insider](https://tech-insider.org/igt-sumsub-vs-onfido-vs-jumio-vs-veriff-for-igaming-kyc-202-en-d169/)) | None |
| Anti-money-laundering (AML) monitoring and sanctions screening | Watches payments for laundering patterns and checks names against government blacklists | A transfer flagged because the recipient's name resembles a sanctioned person | Crowded | None |
| Regulatory reporting | Builds the large structured data returns banks and insurers file with regulators | A bank's quarterly capital report to APRA | Consolidated into a few giants (Regnology and others) | None |
| Regulatory change and horizon scanning | Tracks new and changed rules and maps them to the firm's policies | An alert that a safety regulation changed, linked to the procedure it affects | Crowded and content-heavy (CUBE, LexisNexis) | Low |
| GRC and operational risk | A central register of risks, controls, incidents, audits and owners | The spreadsheet of "top 20 risks" a board reviews, made into software | Crowded at enterprise level (ServiceNow, Archer, Protecht) | Integrate, don't compete |

| Safety (EHS/WHS: environment, health and safety; "work health and safety" in Australia) | Incident reports, inspections, chemical registers, licence and training records | A supervisor's phone checklist for a forklift pre-start check | Busy (Mitti, formerly SafetyCulture; Donesafe; Cority) | Medium-high where it touches rosters and access |

Some prices are public at the self-serve end and almost none at the enterprise end. Third-party deal data puts Vanta's median contract at about **USD 20,000 a year** ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/vanta%23what-does-each-vanta-plan-cost)), Drata at USD 20,000-45,000 for a mid-sized company ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/Drata)), enterprise GRC tools such as LogicGate at a median of about USD 52,600 ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/buyer-guides/logicgate)), and Workiva's enterprise ESG reporting at about USD 388,000 a year ([ERPResearch](https://erpresearch.com/erp-add-ons/esg-sustainability/persefoni-vs-workiva)). Frameworks are commonly sold as add-ons on top of the base price ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/Drata)). These are aggregator estimates built from a limited number of purchases, not list prices.

### What buyers actually ask for

Across surveys and reviews the wish list has converged on four things. First, **automatic, continuous evidence** gathered through deep connections ("integrations" or "connectors", the plumbing that lets one program read another's data through its API, its published programming interface). Second, **"test once, comply many"**: one control, such as "encrypt laptops", counted towards several frameworks at once. Third, clear **board-level reporting**. Fourth, increasingly, **AI that does the legwork while people keep the judgement**. More than 80% of companies in PwC's 2025 global survey plan to use technology to automate compliance ([Yam News summary of PwC](https://n.yam.com/Article/20250407490246)), and 64% of executives in a secondary report named better risk visibility as RegTech's main benefit ([RegTech Analyst](https://regtechanalyst.com/?p=73312)).

The complaints are the mirror image, and they matter more to a newcomer than the wish list because complaints are where buyers will switch. Integration depth is the top complaint about Vanta and Drata on G2, with "integration issues" and "limited integrations" leading the cons ([G2](https://www.g2.com/compare/sprinto-inc-vs-vanta-vs-drata)). Gartner reports that **85% of its assurance clients run more than one GRC tool**, that GRC tools take **more than a year** to reach full use, and that only slightly more than half of enterprise risk users are satisfied ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/5368963)). G2's 2023 GRC report found user adoption of GRC platforms at only **57%** ([G2](https://www.g2.com/articles/g2-grid-report-grc-platforms)), which means many people who are supposed to use these tools rarely log in. A consultancy estimate says GRC reports often take 80 or more person-hours to compile, with most staff time spent chasing spreadsheets ([GRC 20/20](https://www.grc2020.com/?p=390)). The human version of this is a Reddit post from a lone IT and security person at a 120-person company who had a family member help organise audit screenshots ([Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1ptnc81/snapshots/2025-12-23T22%3A00%3A06.813696Z)); one anecdote, but a vivid one. In money-laundering monitoring the pain is **false positives**, alerts that look suspicious but turn out to be fine and still need a person to clear them: in a 2026 vendor survey, **37% of institutions** reported false-positive rates above 50% ([SymphonyAI](https://www.symphonyai.com/resources/compliance-modernization)). The widely repeated "90-95% of alerts are false positives" is attributed to PwC by vendors, but its original could not be found ([Youverify](https://youverify.co/en/blogs/howreduce-aml-false-positives)).

The money behind these complaints is mostly labour. Australia's financial institutions spent about **AU$5.37 billion** on financial-crime compliance (2023 data), with 39% of that on labour and 34% on technology, the highest technology share in the Asia Pacific countries surveyed ([CFOtech Australia](https://cfotech.com.au/story/australia-s-financial-crime-compliance-costs-reach-5-37bn)). For the founder this sets the pitch: hours of checking saved, measured and shown, not a longer feature list.

### Buyers want AI, but they don't trust it to decide

AI is spreading fast: about three-quarters of UK financial firms used it in 2024 ([Finextra](https://finextra.com/newsarticle/45100/ai-adoption-in-uk-fs-sector-grows-but-understanding-lags)). Trust has not kept up. Vixio's 2026 interviews found **65% of compliance leaders distrust generic AI for regulatory decisions** and about 56% require a human checkpoint before acting on AI output ([Vixio](https://www.vixio.com/articles/press-release-new-vixio-report-reveals-65-of-compliance-leaders-distrust-generic-ai-for-regulatory-decisions)). Ethics and compliance leaders named **hallucination** (an AI stating something false with confidence, such as inventing a piece of evidence) as their top barrier at 53% ([Ethisphere](https://ethisphere.com/ai-data-report/)). ASIC warned in 2024 that some licensees adopt AI faster than their governance keeps up ([ASIC REP 798](https://asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/find-a-document/reports/rep-798-beware-the-gap-governance-arrangements-in-the-face-of-ai-innovation/)). Several GRC vendors shipped "agentic" products in October 2026, but an analyst review of 33 providers found that **most agents assist while people verify**, and that the open question is "who verifies the agents" ([Wheelhouse Advisors](https://www.prweb.com/releases/wheelhouse-advisors-finds-ai-agents-at-work-in-grc-but-full-autonomy-has-not-arrived-302900792.html)). Mycelium's rule that AI suggests and people approve is therefore not a handicap. It is what buyers and regulators say they want, and a visible log of "what the AI proposed, who approved it, when" is a selling point.

## A multi-site manufacturer answers to many regulators on different clocks

### Six families of duty, one repeating pattern

A large Australian manufacturer has roughly six families of duty: keep people safe, keep systems and plant networks secure, report emissions and pollution, treat workers and their data fairly, make safe products of consistent quality, and run the company honestly. Nearly every duty reduces to three things: **a register** (what you have: chemicals, plant, licences, suppliers), **a record of doing something** (an inspection, a training session, a review) and **a report or notification** sent on a schedule or when something happens. That pattern is the founder's design brief: Mycelium already holds registers (people, devices, sites, cameras, doors) and records (events, shifts, inspections), so the compliance app's job is to attach them to the right control and the right deadline.

**Safety (WHS)** is the heaviest daily load. The model WHS laws apply everywhere except Victoria, which has its own Act, so a manufacturer with plants in several states answers to several safety regulators (structure from background knowledge, not re-checked: [Safe Work Australia](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/)). Every state now has specific rules on **psychosocial hazards** (risks to mental health, such as bullying or overwork); Victoria was last, from 1 December 2025 ([WorkSafe Victoria](https://www.worksafe.vic.gov.au/news/2025-12/new-regulations-make-psychological-health-priority)). Penalties are serious: the Commonwealth industrial manslaughter offence carries up to **A$18 million** for a company ([AICD](https://www.aicd.com.au/regulatory-compliance/the-psychosocial-regulatory-landscape.html)), and Victoria recorded more than A$17 million in penalties and 137 prosecutions and enforceable undertakings in 2025 ([Safety Solutions](https://www.safetysolutions.net.au/content/business/article/victoria-in-2025-17m-in-penalties-and-137-prosecutions-and-enforceable-undertakings-987785989)). The daily work is registers of hazardous chemicals, registered plant such as cranes and pressure vessels, and "high risk work licences" (for example a forklift licence, valid for five years) plus site tickets and inductions. These details come from background knowledge and should be checked against [Safe Work Australia](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/licences-and-registration) before they reach a customer.

**Cyber** duties that actually bind most manufacturers are narrower than the frameworks suggest. Since enforcement began on **1 January 2026**, any business with turnover above A$3 million that pays a ransom must report it to the ASD within **72 hours** ([Ai Group](https://www.australianindustrygroup.com.au/news/blogs/2025/mandatory-ransomware-payment-reporting/); [Key IT](https://keyit.com.au/news/ransomware-reporting-enforcement-2026/)). The **SOCI Act** (Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018) requires a written risk-management program, the **CIRMP**, covering cyber, personnel, supply-chain and physical hazards ([CISC guidance](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/guidance-for-the-critical-infrastructure-risk-management-program.pdf)), but it binds only owners of listed critical assets. Its food and grocery coverage, for instance, is limited to distribution networks of critical supermarkets and wholesalers, so a food manufacturer is not covered just because it makes food ([CISC](https://www.cisc.gov.au:443/information-for-your-industry/food-and-grocery/legislation-regulation-and-compliance/soci-act-2018)). ISO 27001, the Essential Eight, IEC 62443 (the standard for industrial control networks) and the US NIST Cybersecurity Framework are voluntary for most private manufacturers; customers, insurers, boards or SOCI ask for them. Manufacturers selling into Europe meet **NIS2**, the EU cyber directive, which lists manufacturing sectors such as machinery and electronics and requires an early warning within 24 hours of a significant incident ([Legiscope](https://www.legiscope.com/blog/nis2-essential-important-entities.html); [OpenStatus](https://www.openstatus.dev/guides/nis2-incident-reporting-requirements)).

**Climate and environment** is the fastest-growing duty. Most large manufacturers fall into **Group 2** of Australia's mandatory climate reporting (two of: revenue of A$200 million or more, gross assets of A$500 million or more, 250 or more employees) ([Carbon Impact HQ](https://carbonimpacthq.com/journal/australia-aasb-s2-group-2-climate-reporting-2026)). Their first reporting year began on 1 July 2026 under the standard AASB S2. **Scope 1** emissions (from your own operations, such as burning gas) and **Scope 2** (from the electricity you buy) are required from year one, and **Scope 3** (your whole supply chain and product use) from year two ([Rulesmate](https://rulesmate.com.au/insights/asrs-group-2-climate-reports-1-july-2026)). Heavy emitters already report under NGER (the National Greenhouse and Energy Reporting scheme), and facilities above 100,000 tonnes of emissions face falling Safeguard Mechanism baselines ([Clean Energy Regulator](https://cer.gov.au/document/2024-25-safeguard-mechanism-data-insights)). Exporters of steel and aluminium to Europe are being asked for emissions data under the EU's border carbon levy, CBAM, whose definitive period began on 1 January 2026 ([Customs Support Group](https://www.customssupport.com/cbam-omnibus-i-package/)).

**People and privacy** duties cut directly across Mycelium's own data. The Privacy Act's employee-records exemption survives in the August 2026 exposure draft of the second round of reforms ([Russell Kennedy](https://www.russellkennedy.com.au/insights-events/insights/privacy-reform-tranche-2-arrives-major-changes-proposed-key-reforms-still-missing)), but CCTV footage, contractor and visitor access logs and biometrics are not employee records, so they are covered. Workplace surveillance is regulated state by state: NSW's 2005 Act requires 14 days' written notice before camera or tracking surveillance (from background knowledge: [NSW legislation](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-2005-047)), and Victoria's Labor government has promised notice, consultation and biometric limits if re-elected on 28 November 2026 ([Lander & Rogers](https://landers.com.au/legal-insights-news/vic-government-foreshadows-tougher-workplace-surveillance-laws)). Modern slavery reporting stays at the A$100 million revenue threshold ([AICD](https://www.aicd.com.au/regulatory-compliance/government-legislations/modern-slavery-law/modern-slavery-act-review.html)), and a July 2026 announcement proposed an offence of failing to prevent modern slavery in supply chains ([Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/07/australia-new-modern-slavery-offence-proposed)). It is proposed, not law.

**Physical security** has no single Australian law for a private factory. Its evidence comes from ISO 27001's physical controls (the "A.7" group in the standard's Annex A, which includes A.7.4, physical security monitoring, new in 2022), the SOCI CIRMP's physical and personnel hazards where SOCI applies, and defence-industry security rules for defence suppliers. ISO 27001 auditors ask whether badge logs are kept and reviewed, how long CCTV is kept and who can see it ([HighTable](https://hightable.io/iso-27001-annex-a-7-4-physical-security-monitoring/); [ISMS.online](https://www.isms.online/iso-27001/annex-a-2022/7-4-physical-security-monitoring-2022/)).

### The clocks are short and owned by different teams

One event can start several countdowns at once. A cyber attack that stops a plant and injures someone could trigger a WHS notification "immediately", a ransomware report within 72 hours if a ransom is paid, a SOCI cyber report within 12 or 72 hours if the plant is a listed asset, and a 30-day privacy breach assessment. Each clock belongs to a different team using a different tool. The table summarises the clocks most relevant to a manufacturer. Rows marked "background" were not re-checked this session.

| Trigger | Deadline | Who it goes to | Status of the finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Death, serious injury or dangerous incident | Immediately; written notice within 48 hours if asked | State WHS regulator | Background ([Safe Work Australia](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/safety-topic/managing-health-and-safety/notifiable-incidents)) |
| Ransomware or extortion payment | 72 hours | ASD | Checked ([Ai Group](https://www.australianindustrygroup.com.au/news/blogs/2025/mandatory-ransomware-payment-reporting/)) |
| Significant cyber incident at a SOCI asset | 12 hours (72 for lesser impact) | ASD/CISC | Background ([CISC](https://www.cisc.gov.au/)) |
| Significant incident at an EU NIS2 entity | 24-hour early warning, 72-hour notice, one-month report | National EU authority | Checked ([OpenStatus](https://www.openstatus.dev/guides/nis2-incident-reporting-requirements)) |
| Consumer product linked to death or serious injury | 2 days | ACCC | Background ([Product Safety Australia](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/)) |
| Suspected personal-data breach | Assess within 30 days, notify as soon as practicable | OAIC and affected people | Background ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/)) |

### Where coordination breaks

The failures that recur are organisational, not technical. EY's 2025 study, quoted second-hand, found only about **one in five manufacturers runs a single unified safety platform** ([Pulse/Quality Digest summary](https://www.pulse.bot/manufacturing/news/why-manufacturers-must-transform-ehs-management-now-a62396cd-795e-401d-94e5-358d4c33b52e)), so the same item can be "compliant" at one plant and "non-compliant" at another. One duty has many owners: a forklift licence is a safety duty, stored by HR, checked at the gate by security and used by operations in the roster, and none of the four sees the whole chain. Group-level reports (climate, modern slavery, SOCI's board-approved annual report) are built once a year from site spreadsheets under deadline. And evidence for cyber and physical audits is still mostly a screenshot, even though Intune, access control and camera systems already produce the underlying data. That last break is the one Mycelium sits on top of.

## Most of the map is crowded, and the real gap is narrower than first thought

### Where not to go

Honesty about crowding saves the founder years. **Security-framework automation for software companies** is the most crowded corner of the market: Vanta passed about USD 300 million in annual recurring revenue (ARR, the yearly value of subscriptions) with more than 16,000 customers by April 2026 ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/04/29/exclusive-vanta-arr-300-million-sequoia-shadow-ai-claude-cursor/)), Drata passed about USD 100 million ([geo.sig.ai](https://geo.sig.ai/brands/drata)), and open-source Comp AI raised USD 34 million in September 2026 ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/17/compliance-automation-startup-comp-ai-raises-34m-to-push-into-security/)). **AML Tranche 2 tools** are the biggest new Australian demand event (about 89,557 expected new reporting entities, 39,520 enrolled by mid-August 2026) ([FinCrime Agent](https://fincrimeagent.com/news/issue-14/austrac-section-167-notices-tranche-two-enrolment/)), but at least eight Australian and New Zealand vendors already serve small firms ([NameScan](https://namescan.io/insights/select-the-right-tool-for-tranche-2-aml-compliance/)) and the work is customer checks, which Mycelium's data does not help with. **Carbon accounting** has many local vendors chasing Group 2 ([Trace](https://www.our-trace.com/blog/mid-market-readiness-assessment-dont-delay); [NetNada](https://www.netnada.com/post/budget-2026-27-climate-disclosures)). **AI governance** has well-funded specialists such as Credo AI and Modulos ([Modulos](https://www.modulos.ai/best-ai-governance-platforms/)). **Obligations registers and regulatory-change content** need lawyers maintaining legal content forever, a field LexisNexis already supplies, including as a feed into other GRC tools ([Ansarada help](https://grc.help.ansarada.com/intro_obligations.html)). **Plant network (OT) security** to IEC 62443 needs deep industrial-network visibility Mycelium lacks, and general manufacturing is not one of SOCI's named sectors ([Invicti](https://www.invicti.com/blog/web-security/soci-act-explained-compliance-rules-and-requirements)). And any **psychosocial or right-to-disconnect product that monitors individuals** collides with Mycelium's own no-surveillance rule; at most, team-level overtime and shift-length signals could feed a hazard register as a feature.

### The correction: physical access evidence already exists for big enterprises

An earlier pass of this research ranked "physical security evidence for ISO 27001 and SOCI" as the clearest white space, because it found no GRC platform with native door-access or CCTV connectors. A targeted follow-up check showed that framing was too broad and partly wrong. A layer called **PIAM** (physical identity and access management) sits above one or more door systems and manages who should hold which badge, with approvals, joiner-mover-leaver workflows and periodic **access reviews** (a manager is asked on a schedule whether each person still needs each door, and approves or revokes). PIAM vendors already sell this as compliance evidence. AlertEnterprise Guardian describes itself as "the only native ServiceNow application" for the physical identity lifecycle ([ServiceNow partner finder](https://servicenow.com/partners/partner-finder/alertenterprise-inc.html)). HID SAFE offers attestation audits and "evidence-ready reports" ([HID SAFE Government](https://www.hidglobal.com/ja/products/hid-safe-government)). RightCrowd's Access Audit runs scheduled reviews with digital sign-off and "audit-ready reports with full history and attribution" ([RightCrowd](https://www.rightcrowd.com/solutions/access-audit-management/)). Genetec ClearID schedules access reviews and reports on them across sites ([Genetec](https://resources.genetec.com/en-feature-notes/genetec-clearid-access-reviews)). RightCrowd even frames 2026 as the year "physical access data should be usable as security evidence", which is almost word for word the founder's own pitch ([RightCrowd 2026 trends](https://www.rightcrowd.com/resources/blog/2026-security-and-compliance-trends-a-forward-looking-guide-for-physical-security-leaders/)). A buyer at a large enterprise has probably heard it already.

The same check corrected a second claim. The earlier pass said nothing linked licence status to door access. On Gallagher sites in Australia that link is mature: Gallagher's Rapid Induct integration grants or denies site access based on a person's compliance status in Rapid Global ([Gallagher](https://products.security.gallagher.com/security/uk/en_UK/c/rapid-induct/p/INT158)), and Sine visitor management creates and removes visitor credentials automatically ([Gallagher: Sine](https://products.security.gallagher.com/security/global/en/products/sine-by-honeywell-integration/p/C13078)). Roster tools such as RosterElf say they block shifts when a certificate expires ([RosterElf](https://www.rosterelf.com/blog/staff-licence-permit-certification-tracking-australia)), a vendor claim its own buying guide partly contradicts. Pitching "licence expired, door stops working" as new would cost the founder credibility with Australian security integrators.

### What is still open, stated narrowly

After the correction, five things still look unserved, and together they define the opportunity. First, **mixed vendors and many sites at mid-market prices**: the PIAM products are contact-sales enterprise tools, often built around ServiceNow and aimed at data centres and government, and ClearID covers only Genetec's own ecosystem, so a manufacturer with Gallagher at one plant, Inner Range at another and a cloud door system at a warehouse gets no single review. Second, **evidence beyond "who holds which badge"**: none of the PIAM products found treats camera recording uptime, door-forced and door-held alarms with response times, visitor-log completeness and comms-room inspections as one evidence set. Third, **joining physical evidence to people and cyber evidence per control**: for example one report showing that every person who badged into a licensed area during a shift held a valid licence at that moment and was rostered there, which is what a safety regulator or auditor would want even on a site where Gallagher already blocks expired cards. Fourth, **Australian frameworks out of the box**: no vendor was found mapping to SOCI CIRMP physical hazards. Fifth, **pushing evidence into the GRC tool the customer already uses** rather than becoming another tool: Vanta's integrations page showed no door, badge, camera or visitor integration ([Vanta integrations](https://www.vanta.com/integrations)), and Drata's Envoy integration only reviews who has accounts in the Envoy app, not visitor or door events ([Drata help](https://help.drata.com/en/articles/9769744-envoy-integration-guide)).

The same logic extends to cyber evidence for the factory floor. Intune and Defender data maps onto several Essential Eight mitigations (patch levels, Office macro settings, multi-factor login, admin rights), so a hub that already sees Intune could show **Essential Eight maturity per site** as a live score instead of a yearly consultant's assessment. This is the founder's inference from what the framework covers; Vanta-class tools already read device posture for cloud-first companies, so the difference here is the per-site, multi-entity roll-up for manufacturers, not device checks as such.

## The plug-in has to earn trust before it earns revenue

### Split the work the way mature products do

Every mature compliance product found splits the work the same way: something collects raw data, a compliance layer maps that data to controls and runs scheduled tests, people attest to what software cannot test, and reports and auditor access sit on top. Microsoft's Defender for Cloud dashboard runs automated assessments about every 12 hours and lets users attest manually with attached evidence, showing per control what the customer must do and what Microsoft handles ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-cloud/regulatory-compliance-dashboard)). ServiceNow uses "indicators" that run on a schedule against a data table and raise issues when they fail ([ServiceNow docs](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/Z61H6JNUJXKe8NNPQzK7MQ/5ny9iWZFGcgoawHLtnJZCw)). Vanta lets data it has no connector for be synced as "custom resources" with "custom tests" on top ([Vanta developer docs](https://developer.vanta.com/reference/build-integrations/overview)). For Mycelium the split is natural: **Mycelium collects, read-only; the compliance app maps, tests, collects attestations and exports.** That is also the shape the founder chose on 4 November 2026: a separate application that Mycelium controls as a plug-in, so the boundary between "collect" and "judge", which is what buyers and auditors care about, is also the boundary between the two products.

In plain terms, each control becomes a small record: an identifier, a short self-written summary, and the framework numbers it maps to. Each **test** is a read-only query over Mycelium's records with its own version number, run on a schedule. Each **result** records the control, the test version, the time, pass, fail or not applicable, and references to the source records (identifiers and fingerprints, not copies of personal data, which fits Mycelium's metadata-only rule). Each **attestation** records who, when, what they stated and a fingerprint of the attached file, with AI allowed to draft and a named person required to approve. Results go into an **append-only log** where each entry includes a cryptographic fingerprint (a "hash") of the one before, a "hash chain", so any later edit shows. A hash chain alone does not stop an insider with write access from rewriting the whole chain ([Vapora ADR](https://repo.jesusperez.pro/jesus/Vapora/raw/branch/main/docs/adrs/0039-merkle-audit-trail.md)), so the chain's latest fingerprint should be published somewhere the operator cannot change: a trusted timestamp service, a copy the customer holds, or storage locked against deletion ("write once, read many", WORM) ([archman.dev](https://archman.dev/docs/data-architecture-and-persistence/data-lifecycle-and-compliance/auditing-and-tamper-evident-logs)). This answers a specific auditor worry, that CSV reports "can be easily edited" ([Linford & Co](https://linfordco.com/blog/soc-2-automation-tools-aicpa-faqs)).

For the export format, **OSCAL** (the US NIST's open, machine-readable format for controls and assessment results) has an Assessment Results model and a **POA&M** model ("plan of action and milestones", the list of open problems carried forward), in JSON, XML or YAML ([NIST OSCAL](https://pages.nist.gov/OSCAL/learn/concepts/layer/assessment)). Exporting in OSCAL avoids lock-in and gives a credible standards story to auditors and to any GRC tool on the other side.

### Use only content you are allowed to ship

Which control lists the app can legally include is a product decision, not a footnote. ASD's Essential Eight material is published under **Creative Commons Attribution 4.0** (CC BY 4.0, free for commercial use with credit, excluding the Coat of Arms and ASD logo) ([ASD: Essential Eight explained](https://www.cyber.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-03/Essential%20Eight%20explained%20(November%202023).pdf)); the separate Maturity Model's own licence page was not retrieved and should be checked. The Secure Controls Framework (SCF), a popular crosswalk between frameworks, is CC BY-ND 4.0: you may ship it unmodified with credit, including inside a GRC platform, but distributing derivatives, explicitly including **AI-generated policies built from SCF content**, needs a paid commercial licence ([SCF terms](https://securecontrolsframework.com/terms-conditions)). That clause bites directly on an "AI drafts a policy" feature. ISO standards are sold and copyrighted, so the app should carry only ISO control numbers and short self-written titles, which matches Mycelium's existing rule. CISO Assistant, the leading open-source GRC tool, is licensed under the AGPLv3 (reusing its code in a network-served product obliges you to publish your source), and some frameworks it bundles cannot be redistributed directly ([CISO Assistant README](https://github.com/intuitem/ciso-assistant-community/blob/main/README.md)). It is better treated as a GRC front end to export into than as code to copy.

### Auditors judge; the tool only prepares

Auditors can use tool output but must not rely on it alone. An audit firm's summary of the AICPA's guidance says the auditor "should not place undue reliance on the tool", must understand how each report was generated, and must check the data is current and unaltered ([Linford & Co](https://linfordco.com/blog/soc-2-automation-tools-aicpa-faqs)). The same source says the tool company cannot also perform the audit. Every serious vendor hedges: Wazuh says deploying it does not certify or guarantee compliance ([Wazuh](https://documentation.wazuh.com/5.0-rc/compliance/index.html)), and Microsoft says its reports "might provide evidence" to auditors ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-cloud/regulatory-compliance-dashboard)). The design consequences are concrete: show each connector's last successful sync next to each control (stale data is the auditor's first suspicion), store the query and parameters with every result, sign the export, and give auditors read-only access to source records. The wording consequence is simple: "test passed on 3 November 2026 for 412 of 412 devices", never "compliant".

### Australian legal limits shape the wording

Three legal exposures matter. Only Australian legal practitioners may engage in legal practice under the Legal Profession Uniform Law in NSW, Victoria and WA ([Law Society of NSW](https://www.lawsociety.com.au/practising-law-in-NSW/complaints-and-discipline/unqualified-practitioners)), so the app may say what a public framework contains and what the customer's data shows, but not tell a client what the law requires of them in their circumstances. "Control E8 MFA test failed: 3 admin accounts without multi-factor login" is safe; "you are in breach of the Privacy Act and must notify the OAIC" is not. Business customers spending A$100,000 or less count as "consumers" under the Australian Consumer Law, so consumer guarantees apply and can only be limited, for example to resupplying the service ([HWL Ebsworth](https://hwlebsworth.com.au/a-key-change-to-the-definition-of-consumer-under-the-australian-consumer-law-will-lead-to-more-business-customers-being-able-to-rely-on-consumer-guarantees/); [ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/about-us/publications/a-guide-to-consumer-guarantees-for-businesses-and-legal-practitioners)). Financial product advice under the Corporations Act is irrelevant as long as the product never recommends financial products ([ASIC RG 255](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/find-a-document/regulatory-guides/rg-255-providing-digital-financial-product-advice-to-retail-clients)). Mycelium's own data creates a fourth, practical exposure: cameras, door logs and device data are exactly what workplace surveillance law regulates, so the app should also evidence that notices were given, policies published and consultation held, per site and per state. That is both a compliance feature and a risk the founder carries.

### Buyers judge the vendor, not just the app

Enterprises will send security questionnaires and ask for a penetration test (a paid, authorised attempt to break in) and eventually an ISO 27001 certificate or SOC 2 report for the vendor itself. Atlassian's marketplace now requires a bug bounty or an accredited penetration test before an app gets its "enterprise" badge ([Atlassian](https://developer.atlassian.com/platform/marketplace/atlassian-enterprise-certified-program/)), a good proxy for what large buyers expect of any plug-in. **IRAP** (the Australian Government's security assessment for cloud services) matters only for government data, has no small-business exemption ([Cybernion](https://cybernion-website.pages.dev/insights/irap-for-saas-and-cloud-providers/)) and was quoted by an unverified aggregator at roughly A$50,000-300,000 ([auditxyz](https://auditxyz.pages.dev/frameworks/security-governance/irap)); it is not a first-year cost. Self-hosting is a real advantage here: if the app runs on the customer's own servers and no data leaves the site, the vendor has far less to prove.

Distribution can ride on platforms buyers already trust. Vanta runs a free Integration Partner Program with a Build Integrations API for apps that send data into Vanta ([Vanta developer docs](https://developer.vanta.com/docs/build-integrations)), but there is a catch: custom resources and custom tests, the natural home for door and camera evidence, are reported as available only to private integrations a customer builds for its own account, not to public marketplace listings ([Vanta developer docs](https://developer.vanta.com/reference/build-integrations/overview)). A public listing may be limited to Vanta's supported data types (user accounts, devices, training records, background checks and similar), with physical evidence going in as a customer-configured private integration or as uploaded documents. Multi-site manufacturers are also less likely than software companies to run Vanta at all; ServiceNow, Archer, Protecht, Riskonnect or a spreadsheet are likelier destinations, which argues for OSCAL and plain signed exports first and specific connectors second.

The ranking weighs fit with data Mycelium already collects, how crowded the space is, and how hard it is for one person to enter. It is this report's synthesis, not a sourced ranking.

Pricing should follow the market's habit of selling frameworks as add-ons, but per site may suit manufacturers better than per framework. Renewal complaints about framework add-ons costing thousands each ([Cyber Sierra](https://cybersierra.co/blog/vanta-drata-review/)) suggest room below incumbent price bands. That is an inference, and the founder should test it with the design partner rather than set prices from these notes.

### A practical first step

Build the first version as a **read-only evidence pack for one site of the design partner**, on the fictional demo data first and on real data only once that company's IT has approved a pilot. Scope it to about a dozen controls: four or five Essential Eight checks from Intune (patch age, macro settings, multi-factor login coverage, admin accounts) and six or seven physical and people checks from door and camera metadata (comms-room badge holders against an approved list, leavers whose badges are still active, camera recording uptime against the site's stated retention, forced and held door alarms with time to acknowledge, visitor-log completeness, monthly comms-room inspection done). Each control carries an ISO 27001 or Essential Eight identifier and a self-written one-line summary, never standard text, with ASD credited under CC BY 4.0.

Each test is a versioned, read-only query that Mycelium runs on a schedule. It writes a dated result with source references and fingerprints, not copies, into a hash-chained log whose daily head the customer keeps. Where software cannot decide, such as "is this person's comms-room access still justified?", the AI drafts a suggested answer from the evidence, and a named manager approves or rejects it. The log records both the suggestion and the decision. Nothing in this first version changes a door, a device or an account; it only reads and reports. Reports use wording like "11 of 12 tests passed on 3 November 2026; 1 needs attention" and carry a footer stating that the app prepares evidence, does not certify compliance and is not legal advice. Export as an OSCAL Assessment Results file plus a signed PDF or Markdown pack.

Then do the three things that settle the biggest unknowns cheaply. Show the pack to one ISO 27001 auditor and ask whether they would rely on it, and on what conditions. Ask RightCrowd, Genetec and the design partner's own door vendor directly whether their products already cover camera health, alarms and visitor logs as evidence, and whether they support the customer's other door systems. And ask the design partner which GRC tool, if any, the evidence should land in, before building any specific connector. If the auditor says yes and the vendors say no, rank 1 is real. If the auditor says no, the founder has lost a few weeks rather than a year.

## Conclusion

The research changes the founder's question. "Is there an empty corner of RegTech?" has a discouraging answer: almost every named segment has a funded leader, and even the physical-security corner that first looked empty is already sold to large enterprises. "Which evidence can only someone with Mycelium's data produce cheaply?" has a more encouraging one. The defensible asset is not a framework library or legal content, which others own and maintain, but the joins: the same person, door, shift, device and site seen together across vendors and states. Incumbents are structurally bad at that because each owns only one of those systems. That also suggests the right relationship with the giants: supply the evidence they cannot see, in their formats, rather than fight them for the dashboard. Acquisitions in this market go to exactly that kind of specialist.

The constraints the founder chose for other reasons turn out to be commercial strengths. Read-only collection, metadata only, self-hosting, AI that suggests while people approve, and "never say compliant" line up with what auditors require before they rely on a tool, what regulators expect of AI governance, what Australian legal-practice and consumer law permit, and what buyers say they distrust. The biggest risk is not a competitor but an unanswered question: whether an auditor will accept automated physical evidence. Answering that question with one auditor and one site is worth more than any further desk research.
